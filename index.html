What is the goal?

Just finish aims for a lower peak week and drops the hardest interval session. Beat my best builds more volume and keeps threshold work.

Days per week

Strength, mobility and yoga

Two heavy strength sessions a week is the best supported extra for running economy. Stretching, mobility and yoga have no proven injury benefit, so they are optional and kept easy. Sessions skip long-run and race days, avoid the day before a hard run, and shrink in down weeks and the taper.

Terrain

Trail stretches the goal time by at least 10% and describes long runs by time on feet. With a total climb entered, it uses the climb instead when that is larger.

Hill work

None swaps hills for strides and intervals. Lots adds hill repeats early and long hill repeats late.

A recent result for pacing

A race or hard time trial from the last 8 weeks works best. Illness or a bad day makes every pace too slow.

Rules
Down week every

Weekly distance rises at most 10% per build week. Down weeks drop it 18%. Three or four runs a week need a higher long run share, so the app raises it for you.

Taper length (weeks, race week included)

Research favors tapers of up to 3 weeks that cut distance by 41 to 60% and keep the hard sessions. Auto picks 1 week up to 15 km, 2 weeks up to 30 km, and 3 weeks beyond that. The study evidence comes from shorter events, so for a 50k this is an extension of it.

Your first plan

Open Setup. Pick your race date and goal distance, choose Just finish or Beat my best, set how many days a week you can run, and enter your current weekly distance. Add a recent race result so the app can set your paces. Tap Build plan. Each run has a Done button, and you can skip or move any run if you are sick or busy.

How the plan is built

  1. Paces. Your recent result becomes a fitness score (VDOT). Every training pace is a fixed share of that score. The goal time is projected from the same result. On trail the goal time is stretched by at least 10%, or by the climb if that is larger.
  2. Weekly distance. It starts at your current weekly distance and rises by at most 10% a week toward a peak week. The peak depends on the goal distance and on whether you want to finish or race it.
  3. Down weeks. Every fourth week drops by about 18% so your body can absorb the work.
  4. Long run. It grows by at most 1.5 km a week and stays under 30% of the week (a little more when you run only 3 or 4 days). Days next to the long run are rest or short easy runs.
  5. One hard session a week. Early weeks use hills, strides and short aerobic intervals. Later weeks move to threshold, tempo and goal-pace work. Finish plans drop the threshold intervals. Distance and intensity never peak on the same day.
  6. Taper. The last one to three weeks cut distance by roughly 40 to 60% while keeping some intensity. The last long run sits at least six days before the race.

What it rests on

Reasonably well supported.

  • Training paces from VDOT, from Jack Daniels' Daniels' Running Formula. Based on lab-derived equations and population averages, so your own numbers may differ.
  • Race time projection with the Riegel formula (1981). It tends to flatter runners with a weak endurance base, and it gets less reliable beyond a marathon. The app uses a larger exponent for ultras, which is my own adjustment.
  • Taper shape from the meta-analysis by Bosquet and colleagues (2007): cut volume, keep intensity and frequency. A three-week taper for a 50k stretches evidence gathered on shorter events.

Associations, not proof. The minimum weekly distances come from cohort studies and coaching guidance, with no controlled trials behind them:

  • Half and full marathon volume and performance, 997 runners
  • Ultramarathon training load and injury
  • Science of Ultra mileage minimums (coaching guidance)

Convention with little direct evidence. The 10% weekly rule, a down week every fourth week, the 30% long-run cap, and hills early with race-specific work late are standard coaching habits. A 2025 study of 5,205 runners found no link between weekly increases and injury, but did find more injuries when one run was much longer than the longest run of the past month (study). That is why the long run grows slowly.

My own choices. The 10% minimum trail stretch, counting 100 m of climb as about 1 km of effort, the easy-pace range, the hill-per-km categories, and the watch zones sent to COROS. Treat these as starting points and adjust them.

Strength, mobility and yoga

Strength is the one with evidence. Meta-analyses of trials find that heavy resistance training improves running economy in distance runners, and that heavy lifting works at least as well as plyometrics (review). Individual trials disagree on size of effect (example), so treat the benefit as modest. The app uses up to three sessions a week (two is the usual choice), lighter and higher-rep early, heavier and lower-rep later, then fewer sets in down weeks and the taper, and none in race week.

Stretching and mobility have little evidence. Large reviews, including a Cochrane review of 25 trials, find no reliable effect of stretching on injury, and static stretching right before a run may slightly worsen economy. The routines are short, gentle and optional, for comfort and range of motion.

Yoga evidence is thin. It is offered as an easy recovery option. The app claims no performance or injury benefit for it.

My own choices. The exercise picks, set and rep ranges by phase, the placement rules (nothing the day before a hard run), and the light jumping added for experienced lifters in build weeks.

Your sync code

A sync code is a personal key to your own plan. It lets you open the same plan on your phone, your laptop, or after you clear your browser. Without it, your plan lives only in the browser where you built it.

  • Everyone gets their own. Go to Setup, scroll to Sync, and tap Create my sync code. The app makes a new random code on your device. It never matches anyone else's.
  • Save it. Put it in your notes or a password manager. If you lose it, your plan still works on the device where you made it, but you cannot open it anywhere else.
  • Keep it private. Anyone who has your code can read and change your plan. Never share it, and never ask someone for theirs.
  • Use it on a second device. Open the app there, go to Setup, scroll to Sync, type your code under "Already have a code?", and tap Use this code. This replaces the plan on that device with your saved one.
  • It saves by itself. A few seconds after you tick a run, skip one, or change your setup, the app saves it. You do not need to press anything.

What is saved. Your plan settings, skipped and moved runs, and which runs you finished. Your COROS sign-in is never saved this way. Connect COROS separately on each device.

Where it lives. Saved plans sit in a small database run by the person who shares this app with you. They can see the saved data, which holds run distances and dates, and no names or emails. Only a scrambled version of your code is stored, so the code itself is not in the database. That database is on a free plan and can pause if nobody uses it for a week. Your plan stays on your device either way, and it syncs again once the database is back.

Limits

This is a planning tool, not a coach or a medical service. Paces are estimates from one race result, so retest with a recent time trial now and then. Stop and see a clinician for sharp or lasting pain. If you miss a week through illness, skip it and carry on. Do not try to make up the distance.
